## 1. Felicity Ingram's pixel resolve (and Codrops' recreation)
- URL: https://www.felicityingram.com/ ; recreation https://tympanus.net/Development/ImagePixelLoading/ ; article https://tympanus.net/codrops/2023/05/24/on-scroll-pixelated-image-loading-effect/ ; code https://github.com/codrops/ImagePixelLoading/
- Source: Codrops Playground (Manoela Ilic, 2023), after the photographer's own site. Seen live in the browser: both the original and the demo.
- What: The window opens as a grid of about eight by five flat colour blocks, nothing else; they are the photograph at eight pixels wide, so the sky is already blue and the dress already gold. Within roughly a second the blocks halve, halve again and again (each step a hard cut, no blend), and the last step is the sharp photograph filling the window. The technique is a canvas with image smoothing off, drawing a tiny copy of the image at full size and stepping the copy's size up.
- Why: It is the only opening in this round that starts from the photograph's own colours and never adds a foreign object. A stranger sees abstract colour fields, then feels them snap into a picture; the snap is the moment. It is also deeply "camera": the picture resolves the way a raw file previews.
- Quality: Stepped, not eased: a reveal built from a handful of hard cuts, each one the photograph at a higher resolution, so the picture is present from the first frame as colour and arrives as detail.

## 2. North by Northwest title sequence (Saul Bass, 1959)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/north-by-northwest/
- Source: Art of the Title. Read the write-up; the sequence itself is well known.
- What: A flat green field; thin diagonal lines slide in and cross, making a grid; the credits ride up and down the lines like lift cars. Then the green dissolves away and the grid turns out to be the glass façade of a Manhattan office tower: the lines were the building's mullions all along, with traffic reflected in the glass. The camera finally drops to street level and the crowd.
- Why: The eye is fooled on purpose. What read as pure graphic design was the geometry of a photograph waiting underneath; the dissolve that proves it is the moment people remember sixty-five years later. It moves from "cold abstraction to perceived reality", in the write-up's words.
- Quality: Draw the photograph's own structure first (its horizon, ridge, window frame) as thin lines on a flat ground, then dissolve the ground away so the lines are seen to belong to the picture. The drawing is the picture's skeleton, never decoration on top of it.

## 3. True Detective season one main title (Elastic, 2014)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/true-detective/
- Source: Art of the Title, with Patrick Clair's own notes. Read the write-up.
- What: Black. A silhouette (a head, a torso, a figure by a road) is cut out of the dark, and inside it is a landscape: refinery flares, a flooded field, a highway at dusk. The figure never moves much; the landscape inside it drifts slowly, because footage was slowed to a tenth of its speed and stills were projected on simple 3D shapes and crept past by a virtual camera. One silhouette hands over to the next; by the end the sequence has gone from light to dark and burns out.
- Why: Double exposure is the oldest photographer's trick and it still stops people: a landscape seen through the shape of something else is two pictures for the price of one, and the dark around the cut-out makes the landscape glow. Clair: "we were inspired by photographic double exposures".
- Quality: The silhouette as the window: the photograph is first shown through a cut-out shape on a dark stage, drifting very slowly inside it, and the stage then falls away so the whole photograph remains. A shape, not a frame, is the mask.

## 4. "Transition : Burning" (aiekick, Shadertoy, 2016)
- URL: https://www.shadertoy.com/view/ltV3RG (built on a GLSL Sandbox burning-flag sketch)
- Source: Shadertoy. Read the 54 lines of code and watched it run. WebGL, so take the shape of the wipe, not the code.
- What: Picture A fills the window. A ragged edge starts eating in from one side; the edge is noise (fractal Brownian motion), so it advances as a torn, irregular front, not a straight line. Just ahead of the front the first picture darkens and browns like scorched paper; the front itself is a thin bright rim; behind it picture B is already there, untouched. It takes about two seconds to cross.
- Why: Everyone knows what burning paper looks like, and it carries its own timing: slow to catch, then a run. The torn edge is organic where almost every web wipe is geometric, and the dark scorch ahead of the rim gives the eye something to follow. On a site whose idea is a printed journal, paper giving way to the photograph behind it is literal in the best way. (It is paper burning, not film: the owner's cut "exposure/light leaks" was film brightness, this is a travelling edge.)
- Quality: A noise-shaped front: a wipe whose edge is irregular and advances at an uneven pace, with a narrow band of change (darken, rim) just ahead of it, so the reveal reads as a material event rather than a mask animation. Buildable with a CSS mask from a pre-made noise PNG and a threshold step in canvas.

## 5. Choonsik's Diary: the flat page that was a 3D set (Kakao, FWA of the Month)
- URL: https://lr.doesbook.kr/en/ ; breakdown https://offscreencanvas.com/issues/webgl-intro-animations/
- Source: Offscreen Canvas, "WebGL intro animations" (the "UI to world camera rotation" entry). I saw the opening page live (grid paper with a drawn cat and a checkbox) but the turn did not fire under automation, so the reframe is from the written breakdown.
- What: The site opens as an ordinary centred web page on squared paper: a title, two lines of text, a sticker, a checkbox. Click, and the camera that was looking straight down at the page tilts and pulls back; the page turns out to be one surface in a 3D room, and the elements that looked flat sit at different depths. The breakdown calls it a "seamless camera transition" that "expands the viewer's understanding of the site as a 3D space".
- Why: It is a surprise that the visitor earns by assuming the usual. Nothing flashes; the thing you took for a page is suddenly a place. That is the strongest kind of opening: a change of understanding rather than a change of picture.
- Quality: The reframe: begin as a perfectly ordinary flat page and let the opening consist of one camera move that proves it was a scene with depth. In plain CSS this is a 3D transform on a stage with two or three layers at different translateZ, turned once from flat to oblique and back.

## 6. Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse, main-on-end titles (Alma Mater, 2018)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/spider-man-into-the-spider-verse/ ; ink notes: https://paper.design/blog/retro-print-cmyk-halftone-shader
- Source: Art of the Title (Alma Mater's own account); the film's look is widely documented. Read the write-up; I know the film. The paper.design page is a static WebGL halftone, read only for its notes on ink.
- What: Every scene is treated as if it had been screenprinted: halftone dots, Ben-Day and Kirby dots, spray splatter, and colour plates that sit slightly out of register, so a cyan and a magenta fringe hang off every edge (the film uses that misregistration where other films use blur). Alma Mater rendered in C4D and "batched them through Photoshop" with a printmaking plugin so it looks "as though each scene were screenprinted". Cuts land with flashes of printed sound effects; the final half minute piles reflections and dots until it is a surreal landscape. The paper.design notes add two things a print really has: inks are warmer than pure CMYK, and the press jitters a little every time it makes a mark.
- Why: It is the only widely seen title sequence whose medium is printing rather than film or screen, and misregistration is the one print flaw everybody recognises at a glance: the picture is there but its colours have not yet met. On a site called The Printed Travel Journal, paper white under ink is literally the page.
- Quality: The photograph is printed, not displayed. It arrives as its own separate ink plates, each a flat single colour laid down in turn, a few pixels out of register, and the last beat is registration snapping true, after which it is simply the photograph. Buildable with three or four copies of the image, each reduced to one channel by an SVG feColorMatrix, mix-blend-mode multiply over the paper, translated a few pixels and then to zero. Nothing is written on it; the snap into register is the hand-over.

## 7. WAAPI Vestaboard: the split-flap departure board (Jhey Tompkins, Craft of UI)
- URL: https://codepen.io/jh3y/pen/dPyBXOG (Web Animations API version; GSAP version https://codepen.io/jh3y/pen/yyLmeJd) ; write-up https://craftofui.substack.com/p/time-travel-with-javascript
- Source: CodePen and the Craft of UI write-up. I opened the pen in the browser and saw the board settled ("JUST SHIP IT" on a dark wall); my screenshots missed the flips themselves, so the motion is from the write-up and the code's configuration (perspective, length, 1-second linear flips, stagger set by padding).
- What: A wall of dark cells. On play, cells begin to flip: each flap is hinged across its middle, the top half falls forward and the next character's lower half rises behind it, and a cell runs through several wrong characters before it lands on the right one. Cells start at different moments, so the activity travels across the board as a wave and dies out from one side to the other; then everything is still. The trick: one looping animation holds every character, and the code scrubs its playhead forward, so the board never fakes a flip.
- Why: Everyone who has stood under a Solari board knows the sound before the word; a split-flap reveal makes you hear the clatter with your eyes. It is the one piece of motion that already means "departures": travel's own typeface of arrival. And a board that settles is an authored moment with a clear end.
- Quality: The mechanical cascade: a reveal in which each part visibly passes through wrong states before landing on the right one, with a stagger that runs across the window in a direction, so the final stillness is felt as a settling, not a fade. The stagger-with-intermediate-states is the thing; the cells need not be a grid of tiles (the router has already cut mosaics).

## 8. Rain & Water Effect Experiments (Lucas Bebber, Codrops, 2015)
- URL: https://tympanus.net/Development/RainEffect/ ; article https://tympanus.net/codrops/2015/11/04/rain-water-effect-experiments/ ; code https://github.com/codrops/RainEffect
- Source: Codrops. Seen running in the browser (demos 1 and 2) and read the article.
- What: A city at dusk seen through a window pane. The pane is covered in drops: the big ones refract the scene behind them upside down, slide, and leave a thin trail; small drops nearby merge into bigger ones and fall when they get heavy; the scene behind the glass is soft, as through wet glass. Nothing is revealed in the demo; it is a standing state, WebGL on a canvas, with the drop physics on the CPU. A second demo puts the same pane over a phone screen; a third over video.
- Why: It is one of the demos people still send each other ten years on, because it is not an effect laid over a picture but a second surface between you and the picture, with its own behaviour. The eye stops at glass before it reads what is behind it.
- Quality: Show the medium the photograph was taken through before the photograph. His opening picture was shot through a car window: the pane (drops, a fogged breath, the glare on glass) can be the first thing seen and the photograph what is left when the pane is cleared, in one quick clearing event rather than a slow dissolve. The glass is in front of the picture and leaves; nothing is written on the picture. Buildable in 2D canvas or SVG without refraction; the demo's slow ambient mood is not the thing to borrow, the clearing is.

## 9. In Pieces: thirty endangered species in thirty triangles (Bryan James, 2015)
- URL: http://www.species-in-pieces.com/ ; making-of https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2015/06/the-making-of-in-pieces/
- Source: Smashing Magazine making-of and the project's coverage (Awwwards site of the month, Motionographer). The site itself would not load for the fetch tool today; the motion is from the making-of and the published code.
- What: Thirty triangles, each a div clipped by a three-point CSS clip-path, in flat colours on a flat ground. Together they are an animal. Move to the next species and the same thirty triangles slide and reshape to new coordinates: durations around 0.7 s, delays stepped from 0.2 s so only a few pieces move at once, the movement travelling left to right or right to left with the direction of browsing, all on one curve, cubic-bezier(.7,.3,0,1). The old creature comes apart into pieces that are already becoming the next one; "What's the threat?" smashes it.
- Why: Thirty pieces is few enough to count and large enough to watch; the eye follows individual triangles across and sees the picture assemble, which a thousand particles never allows. The hand-counted economy is why it won the awards it won with no WebGL at all.
- Quality: Few pieces, large, one move each, and a stagger that has a direction: an assembly whose parts can be counted on two hands, where the order of movement runs across the window like a wind, and every piece shares one easing. It is a cousin of the cut slices and mosaic (flat pieces becoming a picture), so the router must weigh that; what it holds that they did not is the counted economy and the travelling order.

## 10. Dynamic Shape Overlays with SVG (Yoichi Kobayashi, Codrops, 2017)
- URL: https://tympanus.net/Development/ShapeOverlays/ ; article https://tympanus.net/codrops/2017/10/17/dynamic-shape-overlays-with-svg/ ; code https://github.com/ykob/shape-overlays
- Source: Codrops. Seen running in the browser (demo 1, opened and closed) and read the article.
- What: A dark page with one word. Press the menu button and three sheets pour down from the top edge, pale grey over dark grey over near-black, each one a wavy curve: an SVG path with 18 control points, every point given its own delay (up to 300 ms) so the edge rolls rather than drops, and each sheet starting 60 ms after the one before, 600 ms per sheet. Within about a second the top sheet is a flat rectangle and the page has become the menu. Close it and the sheets roll back up in the same order. Nothing fades; everything is a surface arriving or leaving.
- Why: Where almost every page transition is an opacity change, this one is material: three overlapping sheets with liquid edges that the eye reads as weight and depth without any 3D. It is the strongest plain-SVG answer to "how does the stage leave?"
- Quality: The stage exits as sheets, not as opacity. A dark stage before the photograph can leave as two or three overlapping flat sheets in the site's own earth colours (clay, olive, slate already exist as whole flat blocks), each edge a wave because its points are delayed differently, pulled off upward or sideways in under a second, with the photograph untouched behind them. This is an exit, not a window: the sheets never frame the picture, they only leave it.

## 11. Koyaanisqatsi: cloud shadows crossing the land (Ron Fricke, 1982)
- URL: https://www.filmsite.org/koyaanisqatsi.html (shot list; the film itself on Criterion)
- Source: Filmsite's shot-by-shot, Wikipedia, and I know the film. Not watchable here; described from the shot list and memory.
- What: After the cave paintings and the rocket, the film's first movement is the desert Southwest in time-lapse: Monument Valley, the Grand Canyon, Lake Powell. The land never moves; what moves is the light. Cloud shadows race across mesas and canyon walls, so the rock is slate-blue one second and red the next, and the edge between lit and unlit is soft, ragged and fast, crossing the whole frame in a couple of seconds, with another shadow following behind. Filmsite lists it as "shadows of clouds rolling over landscapes" and "Shadowy Cloud Patterns (Grand Canyon) - Time Lapse". Later the same shadows cross the glass faces of skyscrapers.
- Why: nothing is done to the picture. No cut, no zoom, no mask; the photograph is complete from the first frame and the only event is the sun coming out across it. The eye follows the moving edge of light the way it follows weather, and the land seems to breathe. It is the thing a landscape photographer actually waits for on a ridge: the cloud to pass.
- Quality: arrival by light, not by reveal. The photograph is present whole from the first frame but under cloud: flat, two stops down, cool. One shadow edge, soft and uneven, crosses it once, in a direction, in about two seconds, and leaves it lit; the lit version is the cover. Different from Fog Line (a veil in front lifting), from Burning (an edge between two pictures) and from Westworld (a line misread): here there is one picture and two lightings. Buildable: the photograph, a darkened copy over it (CSS filter), and a travelling mask on the copy with a feathered, noise-roughened edge (a pre-made noise PNG as mask-image, background-position animated once).

## 12. Reflectance Transformation Imaging: the surface shows its relief as the light moves (Cultural Heritage Imaging; Smithsonian MCI)
- URL: https://culturalheritageimaging.org/Technologies/RTI/ ; https://mci.si.edu/reflectance-transformation-imaging
- Source: CHI's technology page and the Smithsonian description. Read, not run (the viewer is a download).
- What: A fixed camera photographs an object many times, each time with the flash at a different angle; the software computes the slope of the surface at every pixel. In the viewer the visitor drags the light: as it moves, every scratch, tool mark and letter throws a shadow that swings and lengthens, and at a 15-degree raking angle the whole surface stands up in relief. "Specular enhancement" mode drops the colour altogether and shows only shape, as if the object were cast in grey metal (CHI's example: the Sennedjem lintel, shape above, colour below).
- Why: a thing that looked flat turns out to have depth, and it is the light that proves it, not the object; the moving shadows make a stranger reach for the mouse. It is photography used to see a surface rather than a scene, and the relief of a landscape (a ridge, a glacier's folds) is exactly what raking light is for.
- Quality: the picture as relief before it is a picture. The photograph is shown first as a blind emboss on the paper of the page, no colour, its tones read as height, lit by one raking light that sweeps once across the window so the ridge throws a long shadow that swings and shortens; as the light comes overhead the colour arrives and the relief is simply the photograph. Nothing is on the picture; the picture is the surface. Buildable offline in SVG: feColorMatrix (luminance to alpha), feDiffuseLighting with a feDistantLight whose azimuth and elevation are animated in JS, blended multiply over the paper, then a cross-fade to the plain image. A printed journal's own material: intaglio and letterpress are relief on paper.

## 13. 2001: A Space Odyssey, the opening alignment (Stanley Kubrick, 1968)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/2001-a-space-odyssey/
- Source: Art of the Title (entry with the title still and the music credit, no shot list) and Wikipedia; the sequence is in my memory from the film, so the beats below are from knowledge, not from a page.
- What: Black, and the low drone of Also sprach Zarathustra. The camera rises from behind the dark limb of the Moon; the Earth appears as a crescent above it; and exactly on the three ascending trumpet notes a point of light appears at the Earth's rim, swells, and becomes the Sun cresting the edge, its glare flooding the frame on the timpani. The title sits over the lit alignment; black.
- Why: the light arrives over an edge that was already in the picture, at a single named instant, and that instant is one of the most remembered in cinema. Nothing moves except the light, and the frame is never blown out: the Sun has a place and the rest stays dark around it.
- Quality: the crest. A point of light appears at one edge inside the photograph itself (the skyline of Aoraki), blooms along the ridge and floods down over the land in one rising beat, so the dark picture is lit from its own horizon and the lit picture is the cover. It differs from the cut "exposure" (a global white coming down, with leaks) because the light has a position, an edge and a direction and the window never goes white; and from Westworld's lit line because nothing is misread, the ridge is the ridge. Buildable: a darkened copy of the photograph over the plain one, a mask gradient anchored to the ridge's pixel position descending once, and a brief blur-bloom at the crest point; three beats, 3 to 4 seconds.

## 14. The polarising filter: a quarter-turn that takes the glare off the glass (Cambridge in Colour tutorial)
- URL: https://www.cambridgeincolour.com/tutorials/polarizing-filters.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarizing_filter_(photography)
- Source: the tutorial, read in the browser; it has mouse-over before/after pairs, including one of window reflections ("move your mouse over the example to see how a polarizer eliminates the window reflections") and a "No Polarizer / Polarizer at Max" toggle.
- What: Looking out through glass, the pane carries a milky reflection of what is behind the camera. The photographer turns the filter ring; over a quarter-turn the glare drains out and the view behind the glass comes forward, the sky goes from pale to deep blue (strongest in a band at right angles to the sun, so on a wide lens the darkening crosses the sky as a soft band rather than evenly), foliage and rock saturate, haze drops, and on coated or tinted glass a brief rainbow of birefringence can pass through before it clears. Turn further and it all comes back.
- Why: it is one hand gesture that makes the picture truer without moving it or cutting it: arrival by subtraction. A stranger sees a washed picture set like a developing print, but in two seconds and in daylight. It is the landscape photographer's filter, and the owner's own opening photograph is taken through a car window, which is precisely what a polariser is turned for.
- Quality: the turn. The photograph arrives whole but veiled by glass glare, a lifted, low-contrast copy with a faint reflection of the car's interior lying on it; the opening is a single quarter-turn during which the glare drains, the sky deepens as a soft band crossing it, and the colours set. Nothing slides off (that is Big Little Lies' reflection) and nothing is wiped: the veil is extinguished in place, and the band in the sky gives the turn a visible direction. Buildable: the plain photograph, a glare layer (a brightened, desaturated copy or a pale gradient, mix-blend-mode screen) whose opacity and a sky-band gradient are driven by one value from 0 to 90 degrees, with an optional faint hue sweep for the birefringence.

## 15. Slit-scan: the Star Gate's streaks, and the panned landscape (Douglas Trumbull, 1968)
- URL: https://www.redsharknews.com/douglas-trumbull-and-how-slit-scan-changed-sfx ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slit-scan_photography
- Source: RedShark's explainer and Wikipedia, read; the Star Gate is in my memory from the film.
- What: Trumbull's machine took five minutes per frame: the camera crept toward a black mask with a slit, behind which backlit artwork slid sideways, so each frame is one long exposure in which "the horizontal axis represented not a spatial dimension but a temporal one" and the result is "a wall of patterned light stretching into the distance". On screen the picture is pure streak: colours drawn into lines along one axis, nothing nameable, until (in the film's last Star Gate shots and in every slit-scan still) the smear resolves into landscape. The same thing happens in a photographer's hands as a panned or moved exposure (intentional camera movement): the land becomes bands of its own colours, sky, snow, rock, in one direction.
- Why: a stranger sees colour bands that could be a painting (a Rothko of sky, snow and rock) and then watches them contract into a mountain; the recognition is the moment. Streaks read as speed and time, which is the one thing a still photograph is supposed not to have, and a landscape seen from a car at speed is exactly this smear.
- Quality: the smear that sets. The photograph arrives as itself drawn into streaks along one axis, its own colours in bands with no detail, and sets into the sharp picture in one decelerating move, as if the car had stopped. It is not pieces (the smear is continuous), not a travel across the picture (nothing is displaced, the picture is in place throughout), and not focus (the aperture survivor softens; this streaks, with a direction). Buildable with an SVG feGaussianBlur whose stdDeviation is strongly anisotropic (for example 60,0) animated to 0 with one ease, or a canvas that draws the image many times with decreasing horizontal offsets. Closest excluded relative: ripple (a distortion settling); the difference is that ripple wobbles and this streaks along the road.

## 16. The ink blot: the picture is inside the ink (CodyHouse "Ink Transition Effect")
- URL: https://codyhouse.co/gem/ink-transition-effect
- Source: CodyHouse code demo (found through the Codrops hunting ground; Codrops credits the same device). Read the code description, did not run the demo.
- What: (1) A white page. (2) From the centre a black ink blot blooms outward: a PNG sprite of 25 frames of real ink spreading, played in 0.8 s with `steps(24)`, so every frame is a hard cut and the edge keeps the ragged, feathered, bleeding outline of ink soaking into paper. (3) The blot is the window: whatever lies beneath (there, a modal) is seen only inside the ink, and when the ink has covered the window the new view is simply there. Beat by beat: still paper, a drop lands, the stain runs, the stain is the picture.
- Why: the edge is the whole effect. It is not a circle, not a wipe, not a fade: an organic edge that grows unevenly, with fingers and islands, the way ink runs along paper fibres. A stranger reads it instantly as liquid on paper, which no loading bar or iris can claim. Adjacent to, not the same as, the excluded aperture (a geometric iris) and darkroom (the whole picture developing at once): here the picture is sharp and complete from the first frame, and only its visible area grows, along a liquid edge. Buildable without a sprite: an SVG mask of `feTurbulence` + `feComponentTransfer` thresholded, its radius animated, or a prerendered sequence of blot shapes as `mask-image`.
- Quality to borrow: a reveal whose edge is a material (ink in paper fibres), stepped not smoothed, so the growth reads as soaking rather than animating.

## 17. The handscroll: the picture unrolls an arm's length at a time (Chinese 手卷; the moving panorama; Offscreen Canvas "mesh unrolling")
- URL: https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/songdynasty-module/intro-handscroll.html ; the 19th-century western cousin https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moving_panorama ; the web-code sibling https://offscreencanvas.com/issues/dithering-mesh-unrolling-and-postprocessing/
- Source: Asia for Educators (Columbia), the Met's handscroll essay (rate-limited today; the Toledo Museum and Art Institute pages say the same), Wikipedia, Offscreen Canvas. Read descriptions and the article's method, not watched.
- What: (1) A rolled scroll: the viewer sees only the roller and a strip of mounting silk. (2) "Starting at the right end of the scroll, and progressing to the left, the viewer determined the pace", unrolling "only an arm's length section at a time", about fifty centimetres, rerolling behind as they go, so the landscape passes like a journey and the painter builds in "suspense to entice the viewer to keep on looking". The moving panorama industrialised this: a painted canvas up to 9,400 feet long wound between two spools hidden behind a proscenium, emerging from one roller and vanishing into the other while a "Delineator" narrated. (3) On the web, Offscreen Canvas rolls a flat image plane by treating its Y coordinate as an angle: the picture comes off a cylinder, flat where unrolled, curved at the lip, with a progress value from rolled to open.
- Why: it is a wipe that is not a wipe: the picture arrives from a physical object, the roller, with a curved lip of paper that catches the light, and the convention says the landscape is to be read, not glanced at. The right-to-left direction and the rolled paper are foreign to the usual website and native to a travel journal. Adjacent to the excluded book (a codex opening) and the slice wipe (Rear Window's blinds, cut by the router): here there is one edge, it is a roll of paper with thickness and curvature, and it moves at a reading pace. Buildable in CSS: the photograph on a wide element whose visible width grows, with a narrow strip at the lip given a cylindrical shading gradient and a slight `rotateY`, and the roller as a dark bar; no WebGL needed for the flat case.
- Quality to borrow: a reveal paced like reading, from a roll with a curved paper lip, right to left.

## 18. The CRT turning on: a bright band rolls, then the picture comes up out of the dark (Lucas Bebber, "CSS CRT screen effect")
- URL: https://codepen.io/lbebber/pen/XJRdrV
- Source: CodePen, read in the browser (the full SCSS, not run).
- What, read from the keyframes: `turn-on` runs 4 s linear. (1) 0 to 9 %: the screen is a squashed, blown-out band (`scale(1, 0.8)`, `brightness(30)`) that rolls vertically through the frame, down and then up, like a lost vertical hold, widening to `scale(1.3, 0.6)` as it fades. (2) 9 to 11 %: black (`contrast(0) brightness(0)`, opacity 0). (3) 11 to 100 %: the picture itself fades up from black over about 3.5 s, ending slightly hotter than normal (`brightness(1.2) saturate(1.3)`), with a 2-pixel scanline overlay and a flicker pseudo-element whose opacity is random at twenty keyframes. The companion `turn-off` is the classic collapse: 0.55 s, the picture squashes to a bright line (`scale(1.3, 0.001)`, `brightness(10)`) and then to a dot. Its reverse, dot to line to picture, is the folk-memory "turn on" that the pen deliberately replaces with the rolling band.
- Why: it is a device with a known sound. Everyone over thirty has watched a tube warm up; the rolling band is the half-second of chaos before the picture settles, and the slow bloom after it is the tube's own time. Two beats of different speed (a violent 0.4 s, a patient 3.5 s) make an authored rhythm, not an ease. Not a camera and not paper, so it is the find most unlike this site; the router decides. Not an excluded design (no iris, no slices, no projector gate: a projector drops a slide in with a bounce, the tube blooms). Buildable: `clip-path: inset()` for the line and dot, filters for the bloom, a repeating gradient for scanlines.
- Quality to borrow: two beats at two speeds, a violent short one and a slow long one, so the picture's arrival has a rhythm instead of an ease.

## 19. The rubbing: broad strokes across the paper, and the picture is inside the strokes (Chinese 拓印 stone rubbing; demosthenes.info "Scribble Image Reveal with SVG and Blend Modes")
- URL: https://demosthenes.info/blog/1062/Scribble-Image-Reveal-with-SVG-and-Blend-Modes ; the craft https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/ancient-china-and-first-copiers
- Source: Dudley Storey's tutorial (code read), Penn State on Chinese rubbings, museum pages (Australian Museum, Cleveland). Read, not watched.
- What: (1) The craft: damp paper is laid on a carved stone and "pressed into all the crevices" with a stiff brush; then "a silk or cotton pad dipped in ink is dabbed, or squeezed, over all the raised areas", pass after pass, so the picture appears where the pad has been and nowhere else, "white characters on a black background", and pilgrims "would interrupt their journeys long enough to take rubbings" to carry home. (2) The web version: an SVG polyline with a 200-pixel stroke zigzags across the picture; `stroke-dasharray` and `stroke-dashoffset` at 20000 hide it; `stroke-dashoffset` animates to 0 over 3 s linear, and with `mix-blend-mode: lighten` (or, cleaner, the polyline as a `mask`) the photograph is visible only inside the stroke that has been drawn so far. Beat by beat: blank paper; a broad stroke crosses it and the mountain is inside the stroke; the next pass below it adds the glacier; six or seven passes and the sheet is full.
- Why: a hand is visibly at work, and each stroke carries a whole slab of the picture, so the photograph arrives in large honest pieces rather than fading, pixelating or sliding. It is the opposite of ridgeline (which draws the picture's own thin lines; here the stroke is a window, broad and dumb, and the picture inside it is already whole). Not a wipe either: the strokes overlap, turn back on themselves and leave gaps that fill late. Adjacent to nothing on the excluded list. The pad's rhythm, dab-dab-dab, could be kept as `steps()` on the dashoffset.
- Quality to borrow: the picture delivered by strokes of a visible hand, each stroke a window, not a drawing.

## 20. Hand-tinting: the photograph is there in black and white, and colour is brushed on, one area at a time (Yokohama shashin, 1860s to 1900)
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand-colouring_of_photographs ; the collection https://madparis.fr/Presentation-9449
- Source: Wikipedia, Musée des Arts Décoratifs (nearly 700 hand-coloured Japanese travel photographs, Kusakabe Kimbei among them). Read, not watched.
- What: (1) The albumen print is complete and sharp in sepia monochrome. (2) Colourists, trained as watercolourists and woodblock printers, brush transparent colour onto one area at a time, sky, then foliage, then a robe, building dyes "up with repeated washes rather than being applied all at once", preferring "more transparent pigments, since they ensure greater visibility of the photographic image"; watercolour "often leave[s] a darker edge of colour at the boundaries of the painted area", so the wash sits a hair outside the photographed edge. (3) The finished print was sold to travellers as the souvenir of a journey, the ancestor of the travel photograph. Beat by beat: the Aoraki photograph arrives in warm monochrome; the sky takes its blue in one wash; the snow stays white; the tussock takes ochre; the glass of the car window takes its faint green; and with the last wash the picture is the colour photograph.
- Why: nothing is hidden and nothing moves; the picture is whole from the first frame and the event is colour entering it by regions, with a brush's slight overshoot at each edge, which a stranger reads as a hand at work on a print. Distinct from scout 1's false-plate-to-true-plate (one global filter relaxing) and from the excluded darkroom (developing from black) and register (plates sliding): here each region is a separate decision with its own moment. Buildable: the colour photograph beneath a desaturated copy; soft-edged SVG mask shapes, one per region, grow or fade in sequence to cut the grey copy away, each with a slightly darker rim.
- Quality to borrow: colour arriving area by area as washes, each with a brush's soft overshoot, over a photograph that was already whole.

## 21. The woodblock print: the picture is built impression by impression, each colour a flat shape, the line last or first (ukiyo-e; Hiroshige's landscapes)
- URL: https://education.asianart.org/resources/the-ukiyo-e-woodblock-printing-process/ ; the registration marks https://viewingjapaneseprints.net/texts/topics_faq/faq_making_a_print.html
- Source: Asian Art Museum (San Francisco) education page, Viewing Japanese Prints, the British Museum's Hiroshige blog (403 to a fetch; its summary in search says the lightest colour is printed first). Read, not watched.
- What: (1) The key block prints the line drawing in sumi; "colors were printed one at a time; a minimum of about ten impressions were necessary", a separate block for each, the paper laid into the kento notches so "colors with each successive pass would fall in the proper location", each pass rubbed down with the baren. (2) Lightest colours first, darkest last; the sky's bokashi gradation wiped onto the block by hand. Beat by beat, as an opening: blank paper; the first impression lands as the pale flat shapes of the sky and snow, a soft press, slightly darker where the baren bore down; the second brings the mid-tones of the ridges; the third the shadows; the fourth the dark of the car window frame; and the last impression is not a flat shape but the photograph itself, snapping the posterised stack into continuous tone. Five to seven beats, each a press, not a slide.
- Why: the picture assembles from nothing by flat tonal layers that land in place, which is how every printed travel view before photography was made (Hiroshige's Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō is a printed travel journal). Each impression is a decisive event with a thump to it. Distinct from the excluded pixels (spatial resolution halving: squares) and register (plates present from the start and misaligned, sliding into place: here each plate arrives aligned, one at a time, and nothing slides), and from hand-tinting above (which starts from a whole photograph). Buildable: the photograph posterised into five levels with a canvas pass (or `feComponentTransfer` discrete tables), each level a layer that fades in over about 150 ms with a 1-pixel settle, the true photograph last.
- Quality to borrow: assembly by impressions, lightest first, each landing as one flat press, the photograph as the final impression.
