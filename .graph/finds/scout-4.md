# Scout 4 finds (2026-10-08)

Hunting ground: Hasselblad stories, Magnum Arts & Culture, Internet Archive photobooks, Fonts In Use title sequences, Eye Magazine; then Art of the Title and film-history pages. The Hasselblad and Magnum story pages themselves turned out to be plain (photograph, label, title, byline, standfirst; Hasselblad writes the name across the picture, Magnum does not) and are recorded here only as context. The five below are the ones a stranger would stop for.

## 1. The Yosemite Book (1868): a printed book's opening, leaf by leaf
- URL: https://archive.org/details/GR_4646
- Source: Internet Archive (Newberry Library copy), Geological Survey of California, J. D. Whitney, 28 mounted albumen prints mostly by Carleton Watkins. I looked at the first ten page scans myself.
- What: gilt-tooled green leather cover with an empty oval at its centre; bookplate on the endpaper; two blank, foxed leaves; a half-title that says only THE YOSEMITE BOOK. small in the middle of an otherwise bare page; another blank; then the full title page, the title alone printed in red; then the printer's note and the contents. The photographs do not come until all of that has been turned; each albumen print is pasted inside a single thin ruled frame with wide paper around it, the plate number top right and the caption in small spaced capitals in the margin, and the back of every plate leaf is blank (I looked at plate IV, El Capitan and Cathedral Rock).
- Why: the pacing is the opposite of a web page. Four nearly empty leaves before a single word, and the first word is tiny on a huge sheet. The restraint builds appetite; when the plate finally arrives it is an event.
- Quality: the half-title. One short line, small, centred on empty paper, a whole beat before anything else; the reader turns a page to get to the picture.

## 2. Rear Window (1954): blinds rise on the view
- URL: https://filmsite.org/rear.html (also https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/rear-window/)
- Source: Filmsite's shot description and Art of the Title's listing. Read, not watched here.
- What: credits play on a black-and-white frame of three tall window openings covered by bamboo blinds; the blinds roll up one at a time, slowly, in a single take, and the courtyard outside appears from the top down as each one rises; the camera then tracks out through the open window into the courtyard and the film begins. The protagonist is a photographer.
- Why: nothing is done to the image. The thing in front of it is simply lifted away, and because it is three blinds, not one, the reveal has a rhythm, left to right, before the whole view is there.
- Quality: reveal by removing a physical thing from the room, not by an effect on the photograph; the view was always there behind it.

## 3. True Detective season 1 (2014): landscape inside a silhouette
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/true-detective/
- Source: Art of the Title interview with Patrick Clair / Elastic. Read.
- What: photographic double exposures: a portrait is the window, and inside the head and shoulders is a Louisiana landscape, refineries, roads, water. The landscapes began as stills by Richard Misrach, projected onto simple 3D geometry so a virtual camera can drift very slowly through them; much footage is slowed to a tenth of its speed. It moves from light to dark and ends in fire.
- Why: two photographs in one frame without a split or a grid; the eye reads the edge of a person and the depth of a place at once. Slow, floating, no cuts to speak of.
- Quality: one photograph seen through the shape of another, the shape being something the photographer owns (a figure, a camera, a window frame), with the landscape drifting very slowly behind it.

## 4. North by Northwest (1959): the lines of the page become the lines of the picture
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/north-by-northwest/
- Source: Art of the Title essay on Saul Bass. Read.
- What: a flat green ground; thin lines cross it and form a grid; title blocks slide up and down the grid's columns in rhythm; the grid dissolves into the window mullions of a glass office building in Manhattan, which it matches exactly; traffic is reflected in the glass; the sequence drops to street level and the film starts.
- Why: the abstraction turns out to have been the photograph all along. The viewer is tricked, gently: lines on paper were a building.
- Quality: a drawn structure on the ground colour that the photograph then fits into, line for line, so the photograph arrives by matching rather than by fading.

## 5. Big Little Lies (2017): the coast through car glass
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/big-little-lies/ (titles by Mark Woollen & Associates; description from reviews, e.g. https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/history-of-tv-the-beautiful-and-haunting-big-little-lies)
- Source: Art of the Title listing plus written descriptions. Read, not watched here.
- What: cars driving the Big Sur coast road and across Bixby Bridge; children in back seats looking out; the sea on rocks; figures as shadows behind frosted glass; faces reflected on windows; all cut slowly to one song, ending as the drive arrives.
- Why: the landscape is almost always seen through or reflected on glass, so the picture has a second surface in front of it, and the viewer feels inside the car rather than in front of a screen.
- Quality: the window as a second surface: reflection, a frame edge, a passing smear on the glass that clears to the view. The owner's own opening photograph is already through a car window.

## 6. Prokudin-Gorskii's three-colour plates (1909 to 1915): the photograph arrives in three registrations
- URL: https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/empire/making.html (process), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergey_Prokudin-Gorsky (examples)
- Source: Library of Congress, "The Empire That Was Russia", the "Making color images" page; Wikipedia for the camera and the named landscapes. Read; I have seen the three-frame negatives and the composites before, but did not open the image files here.
- What: one narrow glass plate, about 3 by 9 inches, stands upright in the camera, and the same view is exposed three times in quick succession through a blue, a green and a red filter, one above the other, as three black-and-white frames. To show the picture he projected the three frames through the same three filters, superimposed on one screen, and the colour appeared only where all three sat in register. The Library's reconstruction does the same digitally: three grey layers, each tinted, aligned by hand, "the most difficult step". Where something moved between the exposures (water, a cloud, a person) the three colours do not meet, and a fringe of pure red, green or blue hangs off the edge. Landscapes in the set: the Kama river near Perm (1910), the village of Kolchedan in the Urals (1912), Staraya Ladoga (1909).
- Why: colour is not faded in, it is assembled, and a stranger can see it happen: a grey picture, then a second copy slightly off, then a third, each tinted, and the instant they coincide the mountain is in full colour and the fringes vanish. Three decisive beats, not a dissolve, and the picture is whole and sharp at the end with nothing laid over it.
- Quality: the photograph arriving as three tinted copies that slide into register; the click of coincidence is the moment, and the brief colour fringes before it are the only effect. Buildable with three copies of the photograph, mix-blend-mode screen, and translate offsets closing to zero.

## 7. Abelardo Morell, Camera Obscura (1991 to now): the view enters the room upside down
- URL: https://www.abelardomorell.net/camera-obscura
- Source: the photographer's own site, statement and image titles. Read; the page itself opens on a carousel of the photographs (first a 2022 Lower Manhattan sunrise), nothing animated.
- What: he blacks out a room's windows with plastic, cuts a small hole, and the outside falls across the walls, bed and floor as a dim, inverted picture, the skyline hanging from the ceiling and the sky on the carpet; the first exposures took five to ten hours. Later he added a lens for brightness and sharpness, colour film, and a prism to turn the view the right way up, so the room and the view became "more equal partners". The eye reads the furniture first, then notices that the landscape across it is upside down, then cannot stop looking at the join.
- Why: an upside-down landscape is something a stranger stops for before they know why. Nothing is done to the picture except that it is inverted and lying on a surface; it is the ground glass of a view camera made room-sized, which is exactly how a landscape photographer first sees every picture under the dark cloth.
- Quality: the photograph arrives inverted, dim and lying on the paper of the page, and the authored moment is the single decisive turn to upright and full brightness (the prism, or the photographer lifting his head from the ground glass). One rotate and one brightness ramp; the picture is never touched otherwise.

## 8. Watchmen main title (2009, yU+co with Zack Snyder): the flash stops the world and the moment is a photograph
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/watchmen/ (listing), https://motionographer.com/2009/03/06/yuco-the-watchmen-titles/, https://laylatvterence.myblog.arts.ac.uk/?p=41 (beat list)
- Source: Art of the Title credits, Motionographer, a-g-i.org and two written analyses. Read, not watched here.
- What: about six minutes of staged tableaux from 1939 to 1985, each a scene played in extreme slow motion with "brief pauses", so that every tableau reads as a photograph being taken: the Minutemen lined up for their official group portrait as the press flashbulbs pop, the V-J Day kiss in Times Square restaged, Kennedy's motorcade, the Flower Power photograph, Studio 54, the camera drifting slowly through each frozen instant before it moves to the next; the credits are yellow 3D letters set into the scenes.
- Why: the viewer watches a photograph come into existence: the world slows, a flash, and the instant holds. It is the opposite of a montage of stills; it is one event per picture, and the pause after the flash is the whole drama.
- Quality: a short moving view that slows and is stopped by one flash into the still photograph, the still then holding as the cover. This is not reel (many clips cut hard, then the still): it is one shot, one flash, one stop. The flash is a single white frame, not exposure's slow descent from white.

## 9. Carleton Watkins, Yosemite stereographs (1860s): two pictures that become one
- URL: https://carletonwatkins.org/getviewbyid.php?id=1001249 (Yosemite Falls, 2630 feet, Getty 84.XC.870.46)
- Source: the Watkins catalogue raisonné site and the J. Paul Getty Museum record. Read; I know the format from the originals.
- What: a card about 7 inches wide carries two near-identical albumen prints side by side, taken a few inches apart. Held in a stereoscope, the left eye sees one, the right the other, and the two slide together into one picture with depth: the fall recedes, the trees stand forward. Watkins sold Yosemite this way to people who would never go; it is how landscape photography first reached a public, two small pictures that fuse.
- Why: the moment of fusion is a snap. Two copies of a picture drift toward each other and then, suddenly, there is one picture and it has depth. A stranger watching two mountains become one mountain stops.
- Quality: the photograph arriving as a pair that converge and lock into one, a rangefinder's coincidence; the lock is the moment. Buildable with two copies translated toward the centre and a single hold. Distinct from stack (different pictures popping up) and slices (one picture in strips): it is one picture, twice, becoming once.

## 10. The daguerreotype (1839): a mirror that becomes the picture when it is tilted
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daguerreotype (the Met essay and the Library of Congress collection page refused the fetch today)
- Source: Wikipedia's account of the plate's optics; Daguerre's View of the Boulevard du Temple (1838) as the first landscape made this way. Read.
- What: the picture is on a polished silver plate, so the plate is a mirror; the dark parts of the picture are bare silver and the light parts a fine light-scattering frost. Held flat you see yourself; tilt it a few degrees against something dark and the picture appears, positive, "an apparition in space"; tilt further and it goes negative. The picture, as the article says, does not sit on the surface, it arrives when the angle is right.
- Why: a surface that is not a picture until it is tilted is a trick of the real world, not of a screen, and the turn is a single physical gesture: a plane rotates slightly and the photograph is simply there, full, with nothing laid over it and no fade.
- Quality: the photograph appearing by angle: the window is a dull silver sheet, it tilts a few degrees in perspective, the picture arrives as the angle passes, and it settles flat as the cover. One rotate3d and one opacity step tied to it. Distinct from aperture (nothing opens), exposure (nothing brightens slowly) and ripple (nothing distorts): the plane moves, the picture does not.

## 11. Reuben Wu, Lux Noctis (2015 to now): the landscape appears only where the light has passed
- URL: https://reubenwu.com/projects/lux-noctis
- Source: the photographer's own project page and statement. Read; I know the pictures (Alabama Hills, Bisti Badlands, Goosenecks, Moel Tryfan).
- What: at night, in a dark desert, he flies a drone carrying a strong LED and makes a long exposure while the light moves. The rock is black until the light reaches it; where the light has been, the land is lit and stays lit on the film; the drone's own path hangs in the sky as a thin arc or a halo. The finished picture is a landscape painted in by one moving lamp, with the stars behind. Nothing is added to the scene; the exposure simply accumulates light.
- Why: a stranger watching a black window sees a mountain being painted into existence by a travelling light, ridge by ridge. It is not a fade (the lit part is full and sharp at once, the rest still black), not a wipe (the edge is a soft lamp, not a line), and the picture is complete and untouched when the lamp has finished.
- Quality: the photograph accumulating where a soft light has passed, as in a long exposure; the lamp's travel is the authored moment and the only effect. Buildable with the photograph under a radial-gradient mask whose lit area is painted along a path on a canvas, or an SVG mask of overlapping soft circles added over three or four seconds, then the mask released. Distinct from exposure (uniform brightness), darkroom (uniform chemistry), counter and slices (straight edges) and the circled spot (one spot, with annotation).

## 12. Vertigo (1958, Irmin Roberts for Hitchcock) and Jaws (1975): the frame holds, the depth moves
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolly_zoom
- Source: the dolly-zoom entry (invention, optics, the Jaws beach shot). Read; the shots themselves I know.
- What: the camera tracks back while the lens zooms in (or the reverse) so the subject stays exactly the same size in the frame while everything behind it swells or shrinks: in Vertigo the stairwell stretches away; in Jaws, Brody on the beach does not move while the sea behind him rushes closer. The composition never changes; only the relation of near to far does, over two or three seconds, and then it stops dead.
- Why: it is the one camera move that is not a pan, zoom or pull-back over the picture: the picture's edges stay where they are and the world inside it breathes. A viewer feels it in the stomach before understanding it. For a photograph through a car window, the window and its frame would hold still while the mountain beyond comes up to the glass.
- Quality: depth moving while the frame stays; the stop is the photograph. Answers the Watchmen note: the view moves without footage and without a pan over the still, and it stops by arriving, not by a flash. Needs the picture in two layers (foreground cut out, background behind it, the background scaled from about 1.3 to 1 while the foreground stays), which this repository already has a tool for (cutout.swift). Distinct from pullback (a plain zoom out) and flythrough (nested photographs).

## 13. Boardwalk Empire main title (2010, Imaginary Forces) and the cinemagraph (Beck and Burg, 2011): a still man, a moving sky
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/boardwalk-empire/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinemagraph
- Source: Art of the Title's description and Karin Fong's remarks; Wikipedia on the cinemagraph's origin. Read.
- What: Nucky stands at the water's edge in his suit and does not move, while the sky time-lapses through weather and light behind him and the tide brings a thousand liquor bottles in around his shoes; the surf recedes and his shoes are dry. The cinemagraph is the same idea reduced to a photograph: a still picture in which one small region (hair in wind, a flag, steam) repeats a small motion while everything else is a dead-still photograph; Beck and Burg called it giving life to a marble statue.
- Why: the eye is held because one thing moves inside a thing that should not move. It is the opposite of a video: the frame, the light, the subject are a photograph, and only the weather is alive in it. The stop is natural: the moving part comes to rest and the picture is what it always was.
- Quality: the photograph arriving with only its weather moving (cloud drifting behind the summit, the mist in the valley), everything else already the still, and the motion settling to rest. Buildable with a masked copy of the sky or cloud region translated slowly and eased to zero; no footage, no pan. This is the second answer to the Watchmen note: the view moves because one region of the still moves, and the stop is the drift ending.

## 14. Abel Gance, Napoléon (1927), the Polyvision triptych: the picture widens to three times itself
- URL: https://bfi.org.uk/features/napoleon-highlights-abel-gance-silent-film and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvision
- Source: the BFI's highlights essay and the Polyvision entry. Read; I have seen the Brownlow restoration's finale.
- What: for five hours the film is one ordinary frame. Then, as the army of Italy begins to march, the picture expands to three times its width: two more projectors come on and the screen becomes a panorama 4:1, shot by three cameras stacked and aimed left, ahead and right. The wings sometimes continue the centre as one sweep across the camp and sometimes carry different or mirrored images; at the very end the left and right panels are tinted red and blue so the whole screen is the tricolore. Distributors later kept only the centre strip, so most audiences never saw it.
- Why: the gasp is the widening. A viewer who has accepted the size of the picture is suddenly given two more pictures' worth of world at once; nothing dissolves, nothing moves, the view simply becomes wide. It is the one historical moment where cinema did what a landscape photograph does: it went wide.
- Quality: the opening photograph arriving as its centre third, tall and narrow, held long enough to be accepted, and then the two wings switching on together so the full width is there in one beat. Buildable with three clip-paths on one photograph (the centre first, the outer two together). Distinct from slices (many strips rising in sequence) and grow (one small picture scaling up): here nothing scales or rises; the wings come on at once, as two more projectors.

## 15. Ansel Adams, Moonrise, Hernandez (1941 to 1980): the straight print, then the performance
- URL: https://petapixel.com/2018/11/07/the-story-behind-ansel-adams-iconic-moonrise-hernandez/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonrise,_Hernandez,_New_Mexico
- Source: PetaPixel's account of the printing history and the Wikipedia entry (1,300 prints, the 1948 intensification). Read; I know the early and late prints.
- What: the negative was exposed in a hurry by the roadside. The ten prints made before 1948 show the straight picture: an even, light, flat sky, a grey village. In December 1948 he intensified the negative up to the horizon line, and from then on, print after print for thirty years, he burned the sky darker and dodged the crosses and the moon brighter, until the sky was an inky black and the cemetery crosses brilliant white. He said the negative is the score and the print the performance; by the 1970s he felt the prints finally matched what he had seen.
- Why: a visitor sees the same photograph twice in a few seconds: first as the camera recorded it, flat and honest, and then as the photographer meant it, the sky going down, the ridge coming up, the whites lifted, in three or four decisive regional moves. It is a before-and-after without a split, and the after is the real photograph.
- Quality: the photograph arriving as its straight print (lifted blacks, low contrast, the paper showing through) and then performed, region by region, by the printer's hand: sky burned down, foreground dodged up, the summit given its contrast, each a distinct beat. Buildable with the final photograph under two or three masked tone overlays (multiply for burning, screen for dodging) that fade out in sequence, so the end state is the untouched file. Distinct from exposure (one global brightness ramp from white) and darkroom (the image emerging from blank): here the picture is whole from the first frame; only its tones are decided.
