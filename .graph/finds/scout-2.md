# Scout 2: title sequences (Art of the Title, Imaginary Forces, Sarofsky, Motionographer, Watch the Titles), 2026-10-08

Read descriptions and case texts only, no video. Where a page held only credits, the beats come from other written sources or from memory, and each entry says so.

## 1. True Detective, season 1 main title (Elastic, Patrick Clair, 2014)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/true-detective/
- Source: Art of the Title interview page (read in full).
- What: Portraits of the cast are shot in slow motion, then used as double exposures: the dark parts of a face or torso become the window onto a second photograph, Louisiana refineries, truck stops and coastline (after Richard Misrach's photographs). The landscapes are projected onto low-poly 3D blocks so a virtual camera can drift through them very slightly while the portrait stays still. The sequence moves from light to dark and ends with the images burning away.
- Why: One frame reads two ways at once; the eye keeps switching between the person and the place inside them. Nothing is cut out, nothing is laid on top: it is pure photographic double exposure, an old darkroom trick done with film footage.
- Quality: the double exposure: one photograph's shadows are the only window onto another, and the two trade places as the light changes.

## 2. Dark main title (ARRI Media VFX, Lutz Lemke, 2017)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/dark/
- Source: Art of the Title entry plus the ADC award listing and a written review (the AotT page is short).
- What: Real footage of the show's forest, caves, roads and faces is mirrored on the vertical centre line of the frame, so trees and doorways fold in on themselves like a Rorschach blot, pulled toward or pushed out of an invisible seam in the middle. Each shot holds its symmetry for a second, then cuts to the next; the title appears last, also mirrored.
- Why: A perfectly symmetrical landscape is instantly strange; the viewer stops to work out which half is real. It is made from nothing but the footage itself, folded, and it is unmistakable from a single frame.
- Quality: the fold: a photograph arrives mirrored on its own centre line, and the opening is the seam unfolding so the real half takes the whole window.

## 3. North by Northwest main title (Saul Bass, 1959)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/north-by-northwest/
- Source: Art of the Title page (read in full).
- What: A flat green field is crossed by a grid of thin lines; the credits slide in and out along those lines. Then the drawing dissolves and the grid turns out to be the mullions of a mirrored office façade on Park Avenue, with traffic reflected in it. The camera drops to street level and the film has begun.
- Why: The abstraction becomes the real thing without a cut. The lines were never decoration; they were the building all along, and the moment of recognition is the whole hook.
- Quality: the drawing that turns out to be the photograph: a few plain lines on paper (a horizon, a ridge, a road) that the photograph then fills in exactly, so the lines were its contour all along.

## 4. Big Little Lies main title (Mark Woollen & Associates, 2017)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/big-little-lies/
- Source: Art of the Title entry (credits only) plus a written description of the credits (PureWow) and memory of the sequence itself.
- What: The mothers drive the Pacific Coast Highway; the camera is inside the cars, looking out through windscreens and side windows at the Bixby Creek Bridge and the cliffs, with the women's faces and the children caught in reflections and rear-view mirrors. Shots of children watching through glass alternate with the coast; shadows move behind frosted glass; the group walks the beach. Michael Kiwanuka's "Cold Little Heart" sets a slow, even pace.
- Why: The whole landscape is seen through glass. A reflection lies over the view and the view lies behind the reflection, so every shot has a near and a far layer, and the eye is pulled through.
- Quality: the view through glass: a reflection (sky, the inside of the car, a face) sits on the window, then slides off and the landscape behind it is left clean.

## 5. Dexter main title (Digital Kitchen, 2006)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/dexter/
- Source: Art of the Title interview page (read in full).
- What: A morning routine shot in extreme close-up with a very shallow lens: a mosquito slapped, a razor on skin, an egg cracked, blood orange squeezed, floss, a shirt pulled on, the door closing. Hard cuts in rhythm with a cabaret-like score; the lighting is flat and factual, like crime-scene photography, which is what makes toast look like a wound.
- Why: Ordinary objects at macro scale become unknown objects. The viewer stops because they cannot name what they are seeing until the cut, and the pace never lets them settle.
- Quality: the ritual at macro: the small, rhythmic ceremony before a photograph is taken (cloth on glass, strap, lens cap, window wound down) in extreme close-up, cut hard, until the last cut is the view itself.

Also seen, not carried as a find: The Shining main title (one uncut helicopter glide following a tiny yellow car up a mountain road; the car is the scale key), because its quality is a slow glide close to the pull-back he already found boring.

## 6. The Leftovers, season 2 main title (Elastic, Angus Wall, 2015)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/the-leftovers-season-2/
- Source: Art of the Title entry (read), plus the Elastic credits. Beats from the written description.
- What: A montage of still photographs of ordinary American life: a wedding, a bath, a birthday, a porch, a funeral. In each photograph one person is missing, and the missing person is a silhouette, an outline filled with sky: cloud formations, a starfield, a sunset. The camera barely moves; the stills dissolve one into the next to an Iris DeMent song, and the last frame is left without an answer.
- Why: A photograph that is whole except for one shape, and the shape is a window onto weather. The eye goes straight to the hole and cannot stop reading two depths at once: the room, and the sky where a person should be.
- Quality: the absence that is sky: the opening photograph arrives complete except for its subject, which is plain paper or a different sky until the very last beat, when the mountain itself fills its own outline.

## 7. The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power main title (Plains of Yonder, Katrina Crawford and Mark Bashore, 2022)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/the-lord-of-the-rings-the-rings-of-power/
- Source: Art of the Title credits page (short), Inverse's science piece and TheOneRing's write-up, and the studio's own page at plainsofyonder.com/work/lord-of-the-rings. Beats from those texts.
- What: Fine sand and stone grains lie on a plate. A tone sounds and the grains jump, flee the vibrating zones and gather on the still ones, so a symmetrical figure appears out of loose matter (a Chladni figure). The frequency changes and the figure breaks and re-forms as the next one: rings, curved lines, two facing trees, a dark shape that winds through later episodes. Real sand on trays was filmed first (Crawford found the forms by spreading sand and playing Gregorian chant), then extended digitally. Nothing is drawn; everything is grain that has settled.
- Why: Matter rearranging itself at a sound is uncanny in a way a dissolve never is. The patterns do not morph; one shatters and the next snaps into place, so every change is a decisive beat and the eye waits for the next.
- Quality: grain that is shaken into place: the photograph as loose dark grain on warm paper that, at one change of note, gathers into the ridge, then at the next into its tones, until the grain is the print. Not darkroom's slow emergence in a tray; a snap, physical and in time with a sound.

## 8. La Jetée (Chris Marker, 1962)
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Jet%C3%A9e
- Source: Wikipedia and film-history commentary; a film, not a title sequence, brought back for its opening grammar. Beats from the written accounts.
- What: Twenty-eight minutes made almost entirely of black-and-white still photographs, shot on a Pentax and optically printed, cut and dissolved in a shifting rhythm against a narrator. Nothing moves until, once, a woman asleep opens her eyes and blinks for about six seconds of real motion, filmed on an Arriflex Marker could only hire for one afternoon. Then the stills resume.
- Why: Because everything else is still, the one moment of motion lands like a shout. The viewer has accepted stillness as the rule of the world, so a blink becomes the most eye-catching event imaginable.
- Quality: stillness as the rule, one motion as the exception: the opening is the photograph held perfectly still, and exactly one thing moves once (the view beyond the car window shifting a hand's width as if the car rolled to a stop, with the frame itself motionless), then everything is still again and the page is there.

## 9. Rear Window opening credits (Alfred Hitchcock, 1954)
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rear_Window
- Source: Written descriptions (Jonathan Rosenbaum's "Backyard Ethics", a PSU film-studies paper, teaching notes) and memory of the sequence; no designer interview exists for it.
- What: A dark room; three bamboo blinds hang over a photographer's window. The credits appear as, one at a time, the blinds are raised, each a few seconds after the last, like a theatre curtain going up in three parts, and the sunlit courtyard beyond is uncovered. When the last blind is up the camera leaves the window and travels into the courtyard, and only afterwards back to the sleeping photographer and the smashed camera on his sill.
- Why: The view is withheld and then granted by a physical object the audience understands at once. Three staggered reveals instead of one give it a rhythm, and the dark room around the bright window makes the view glow.
- Quality: the blind going up, in three parts: not a single wipe but three adjacent panels of the window uncovered in turn, each at its own moment, from a dark room onto the lit view (and the window is already in his photograph: Aoraki is seen through a car window). The closest excluded design is counter's single wipe from below; the difference is the three staggered panels and the object that lifts them.

## 10. Westworld main title (Elastic, Patrick Clair, 2016)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/westworld/
- Source: Art of the Title interview page (read in full).
- What: The first shot reads as a sunrise over a landscape: a lit curve against black. As the light moves it is revealed to be the ribcage of a horse being strung from piano wire by robotic arms. The horse is then made to gallop in Muybridge's frozen sequence while it is still being built; a half-formed rider's eyes come into focus; two unfinished figures embrace; a player piano plays itself. Clair: take what is at the core of the Western and put it in "a really stark, beautiful, graphic sci-fi frame", then let the strangeness come from seeing it exposed.
- Why: The first second is a deliberate misreading, and the correction is the hook: you thought you saw a horizon. A second lure follows, the galloping horse that is still being made, so two kinds of seeing overlap.
- Quality: the first frame that is misread: the opening shows a single lit line against dark that reads as the horizon at dawn, and the photograph, as light reaches it, proves that line was its own ridge all along. The borrowed thing is the misreading and its correction, not the threads or the machines.

Also seen this round, not carried: Shōgun (Elastic, Nadia Tzuo, 2024), a raked dry garden in which a ship and the land appear in sand, too close to the map and route family; Mad Men (Imaginary Forces, 2007), a silhouette falling past adverts, nothing in it for a still landscape; Imaginary Forces' Star City (brutalist monuments and tiny figures rising toward one image) and War (abstract painterly forms colliding); Nexus Studios' Playgrounds in Motion titles, an infinite zoom with colour entering a monochrome world, excluded as a flythrough family and too near darkroom's colour arriving; Sarofsky's Superman work (an S-shield detaching and a stark icy landscape intro), typographic.

## 11. Taxi Driver opening (Martin Scorsese, titles by Dan Perri, 1976)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/taxi-driver/
- Source: Art of the Title entry (its description read in full; it holds no Perri quote), a film-studies transcript ("Taxi Driver and New Hollywood", Macmillan) for the windshield shots, and Print magazine on Perri's method (second-unit footage colour-treated by film copying and slit-scan).
- What: Sewer steam rises and a yellow cab comes through it; Perri's neon credits appear on the re-formed mist, "each credit appearing only as long as necessary". Cut to Bickle's eyes, lit red then white, moving slowly from side to side. Then his view: a rain-smeared windshield, neon in red, white and blue dissolved into jittery smears, "a melange of colors and images that remain constant and blurred". The wipers cross like a metronome and for a second the street is sharp, then the rain closes it again. The transcript's line: the eyes shift like windshield wipers.
- Why: The view is there from the first frame but withheld by water, and the thing that grants it is an object everyone has sat behind. The arc is a shape no screen transition uses, and the alternation of sharp and smeared is a pulse the eye waits on.
- Quality: the wiper arc: the photograph is present from the start, fogged or rained on the car glass, and one physical sweep of the blade clears it, the sweep's edge a curve with a wet trailing line, the glass outside the arc still smeared until the second blade passes. Unlike ripple (a distortion settling everywhere at once) the clearing is local, shaped and caused by a thing; and the car window is already in his photograph.

## 12. Lawrence of Arabia, the match cut (David Lean, editor Anne V. Coates, 1962)
- URL: https://www.openculture.com/2019/09/the-greatest-cut-in-film-history.html
- Source: Open Culture's article with Coates' account, and Wikipedia's "Match cut" entry for the term (graphic match: shapes, colours or movement matched across a cut). A film beat, not a title sequence, brought back for its grammar.
- What: Lawrence strikes a match to light Dryden's cigarette and holds it until it nearly burns his fingers; he blows it out; in the very next frame the screen is the desert horizon, dark below, orange above, and the sun's rim comes up where the flame was, slowly, as the music swells. The script said dissolve; seeing the rushes cut together, Lean and Coates kept the cut and she took two frames off the outgoing shot to make it exact.
- Why: A wait (the flame held too long) and then an impossible leap of scale in one frame. Nothing moves the eye; the world is swapped behind a shared shape, so the cut reads as continuation, not interruption. Anthony Lane: if something inside you doesn't flare up at it, give up the movies.
- Quality: the match cut: a small bright shape on the dark stage goes out, and the next frame is the photograph whole, with its own bright shape (the sun, the lit snow of the peak, the pale window) in the same place. One cut, not a montage (burst is many photographs accelerating); the join is the shape, and the held beat before it is what makes it land. Answers the Rings of Power note: a snap, with nothing dissolving into grain.

## 13. Bunny Lake Is Missing main title (Saul Bass, 1965)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/bunny-lake-is-missing/
- Source: Art of the Title entry (read in full) and the Stedelijk collection note.
- What: A black screen. A hand enters and tears a shape out of the black paper; the hole shows a credit on white. Another tear, another credit, each edge jagged. No colour, no cuts, no tricks, the page says: "just the raw, naked concept". It ends with the shape of a girl torn out, as if she were missing from the sheet.
- Why: A tear is irreversible and audible even in silence; the eye follows the travelling point of the rip, not a shape growing. The material is paper, which everyone has torn, and the jagged edge is unrepeatable, so it cannot be mistaken for a wipe.
- Quality: the tear: the paper page is torn open by a rip that travels, with a jagged edge and the lifted flap throwing a thin shadow, and the photograph is what lay beneath the paper. The neighbours are letters (a hole in paper as the window) and slices; the difference is that nothing scales and nothing slides: a tear point travels, and the paper behaves as paper. Answers the Rear Window note: the object behaves as itself.

## 14. Star Guitar (Michel Gondry for The Chemical Brothers, 2002)
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Guitar
- Source: Wikipedia and Campaign's "Music Videos" column; a music video, brought back for its grammar. How it ends is not in the texts I read.
- What: One continuous shot from a train window between Nîmes and Valence. Every post, shed, bridge, pylon and platform passes exactly on a beat, a hi-hat or a synth line, so the landscape plays the song. Gondry plotted it on graph paper, modelled the timing with oranges, forks, tapes, books and tennis shoes on a table, rode the line ten times in a day for different light, and spent two months compositing the pieces into one unbroken view.
- Why: An ordinary window becomes authored. A few bars in, the viewer realises the world is keeping time and cannot stop checking; the motion is large and continuous, never a tremor.
- Quality: the moving window: the frame fixed, the land passing behind it with near things fast and the far mountain almost still (a photographer's parallax), each near post arriving on a beat, the whole decelerating like a vehicle and stopping on the exact composition. The neighbour is filmstrip (a strip races, slows, stops, grows); here nothing grows and there are no frames, the mountain itself hardly moves, only the near layer does, and the vehicle is the one his photograph was made from. Answers La Jetée (the motion is unmistakable) and Rings of Power (the rhythm drives what passes, not grain).

## 15. Verso: the back of the print (Thomas Sauvin, VERSO, 2016; press-print backs; card-mount backmarks 1855 to 1915)
- URL: https://rvb-books.com/products/thomas-sauvin-verso-special-edition
- Source: RVB Books' page on Sauvin's VERSO (ID photographs whose handwritten characters on the back show through the paper), Half Letter Press on news-photo backs (stamps, pencil captions, crop marks), and FotoFocus's "Verso: Backmarks" exhibition of card-mount backs. Not a sequence; a photographer's object, and the one gesture by which a print is read.
- What: A print lies face down. Its back carries the pencil caption (place, year), a stamp, sometimes the picture faintly showing through. The reader turns it over. As an opening: the page is the back of one print at full window, warm paper with a small pencil line in both languages and his stamp; a pause; the page turns about its vertical axis in one motion, edge-on for an instant, and the front is the photograph at full window.
- Why: Everyone knows the gesture, and a whole window turning over is one unmistakable move. The words live where a photographer actually writes them, on the back, never on the picture.
- Quality: the turn-over: the paper page is the verso of the photograph, and the opening is the print being turned face up. The neighbours are prints (dealt, the last grows) and book (a cover opens, the camera moves in); here there is one print, already window-sized, no growth, no camera move, one turn.

Also seen this round, not carried: Up in the Air (Shadowplay, 2009; aerials shot straight down so fields read as pattern, "distant, abstract, detached"), a rotation cousin of Morell's camera obscura on scout 1's list and poor for a photograph with a horizon; Trapped (2015; vertical aerials and 50-megapixel stills given camera moves in post, flesh as soil), a pull-back; West Side Story (Bass, 1961; a drawing whose colours shift through the overture until it is the skyline), the same family as North by Northwest; Smoke (Digital Kitchen, 2025; real burns played backward so each object returns from its ash), a picture assembling itself, which he cut; Young Sherlock (Imaginary Forces, 2026; one continuous move, macro to micro), a flythrough; Gone Girl (Fincher, 2014; credits that fade in and out in three seconds), a timing rule, not an opening; Days of Heaven (Perri, 1978; archival stills, the last one the film's own); Counterpart, Butterfly, Bonjour Tristesse, Babystar, Landman, Master Photographers (BBC, 1983).

## 16. The Pacific main title (Imaginary Forces, Steve Fuller, 2010)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/the-pacific/
- Source: Art of the Title interview page (read; quotes from it).
- What: Rough charcoal and conté marks on textured paper. A stick traces the outline of a soldier, a helmet, a shoreline; charcoal dust scatters off the strokes "like shrapnel" and is blown across the paper. Where the dust clears, the drawing has become black-and-white footage of the same thing, with a red tint; then the next drawing begins. Three paths branch from one horizon line. Fuller on the material: "We tried everything: stomping on it, scraping it across paper with a metal cylinder, blowing it across the surface"; they looked at Franz Kline and Motherwell; the live action was "planned from the start... a missed opportunity if we never saw a drawing come to life".
- Why: The drawing is made of loose matter, so it can be disturbed: a breath moves it. The eye follows dust in flight rather than a dissolve, and the picture under it is discovered, not faded in.
- Quality: the charcoal that is blown off: the ridge and the horizon are rubbed onto the paper in charcoal, in the photograph's own dark tones rather than thin lines, and one gust carries the dust off the page so that what is left under it is the photograph. For the router: this is an alternative execution of the ridgeline CONCEPT he kept (a drawing on paper that turns out to be the photograph), with a different material (tone, not line) and a different reveal (dust blown, not paper dissolving). Not darkroom: nothing emerges slowly; the dust leaves in one gust.

## 17. Catch Me If You Can main title (Kuntzel + Deygas, 2002)
- URL: https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/catch-me-if-you-can/
- Source: Art of the Title interview page (read; quotes from it) and a 2003 Stopdesign note on the method. I read descriptions only.
- What: The figures are hand-carved rubber stamps: Frank, the agent, the stewardesses, each pose a separate stamp, printed one after another onto paper and then composited into flat 1960s colour fields. A silhouette walks from left to right; the ground he walks on extends as a line and becomes a runway, a hospital corridor, a swimming pool; the colour of the field changes with each place ("the colours signal geographical and temporal transitions"). Kuntzel + Deygas: they "used the same techniques as the film's protagonist, by imagining characters in stamp form", and "what mattered was not our know-how but the emotion we could transmit".
- Why: Nothing is drawn or rendered; every figure is an impression, with the slight unevenness of inked rubber pressed on paper, and that pressure is felt even on a cinema screen. A stamp arrives all at once, which is a different rhythm from anything that grows, slides or fades.
- Quality: the stamp: the photograph arrives on the paper the way a carved stamp lands, pressed down in one beat from a small height, the ink full where the pressure is and lighter at one edge, a thin shadow of the block for an instant before it meets the paper, then the block is gone and the print lies flat as the cover. Also a photographer's object (his seal 印章 goes on the back of a print). Neighbours: projector (a slide dropping into a gate, gravity, a bounce); the difference is pressure from above onto paper, no gate, no bounce, no dark stage, and an inked edge.

## 18. Dust in the Wind 戀戀風塵, the opening train shot (Hou Hsiao-hsien, 1986)
- URL: https://reverseshot.org/archive/entry/old/dust_wind
- Source: Reverse Shot's essay, Film Forum's and the Harvard Film Archive's programme notes and a Popoptiq analysis; a film opening, not a title sequence. Beats from those texts.
- What: Black. A small white speck in the middle of the frame, shaky, seems to come toward the camera; it is the mouth of a tunnel and the train is running at it. The mouth grows and the frame floods with the Pingxi valley: forested hills, the track winding through them, a village. Then the next tunnel takes it all away, and the next gives it back, the black intervals and the lit intervals of unequal lengths, until the train is out in the open and the view holds. Hou then cuts to the two schoolchildren inside the carriage reading, so the shot was never anyone's point of view.
- Why: The view is granted and withdrawn by the land itself, in a rhythm nobody chose, and each return is a small shock because the eye has just adjusted to black. It is a Taiwanese landscape seen from a vehicle, which is also what his opening photograph is.
- Quality: the tunnels: the photograph present from the first return, taken away by black intervals of unequal length that grow shorter and rarer, like a train clearing a run of tunnels, until the last one ends and the view stays. One photograph only, never a second picture, so it is not burst (hard cuts between many photographs, accelerating) and not projector (one dark beat); and no frame moves, so it is not a travel. The near risk is grow if the tunnel mouth is built; the thing to borrow is the intermittence, not the mouth.

## 19. Roma, the opening shot (Alfonso Cuarón, 2018)
- URL: https://www.nybooks.com/online/2019/01/12/roma-through-cuarons-intimate-lens/
- Source: The New York Review's piece and several reviews (Jitney Books, San Diego Reader); a film opening with the credits over it. Beats from those texts.
- What: A floor of cement tiles, seen straight down, dry, black and white, for a long time while the credits run. Water sweeps across it from one side and drains back, again and again, with the sound of a brush and someone humming. Where the floor is wet it is a mirror: a skylight appears in it, then a small aeroplane crosses the reflected sky, and the drone of the plane is in the soundtrack. The wet floor is the sky for as long as the water lies; the next sweep disturbs it; the title comes.
- Why: A flat, dull ground becomes a window downward into the sky, and the proof is a thing moving through it. The eye holds the tiles and the clouds in one surface and cannot settle on either; the plane is the authored event, late and small.
- Quality: the floor as mirror: the warm paper is dry and plain; a thin wash crosses it once, and in the wet the photograph lies as a reflection, then the wash settles and the page is the photograph flat and dry. Neighbours: pane (fogged glass cleared by a wipe), whose surface is in front of the picture; here the picture appears IN the surface, by a liquid, and the medium is the ground itself. Not Lux Noctis's light trail (a lamp passing); not obscura (nothing is turned, though a reflection would be upside down, which the router may or may not want).

## 20. The leporello: Every Building on the Sunset Strip (Ed Ruscha, 1966) and the orihon
- URL: https://placartphoto.com/book/339/every_building_on_the_sunset_strip-ed_ruscha
- Source: Placart's and Arcana's catalogue descriptions of the book (25 feet unfolded, 17.8 x 721 cm, the photographs mounted so close that the strip reads as one flat surface, the format taken from the Japanese photobook Ginza Kaiwai). A photographer's object, not a sequence; the opening beats are what the object does when handled.
- What: Closed, it is a small book. Opened, it is an accordion: the leaves pull apart in a zigzag, each panel hinged to the next on alternate sides, so that as it comes out the picture stands in a row of pleats, every other face catching the light, the far ones foreshortened; pull it taut and the pleats flatten into one continuous photograph with the folds still faintly visible as vertical lines. Ruscha's is a 2.5-kilometre street as one strip, both sides of the road, one above the other.
- Why: The whole picture is present from the first pull and the eye reads it twice: first as an object with depth, then as a flat image, and the switch happens in the hand. The zigzag is a shape no screen transition uses.
- Quality: the concertina: the photograph arrives already full-window but pleated, standing in six or eight vertical panels hinged alternately toward and away from the viewer, each face lit a little differently, and one pull flattens them into the plane of the page, where the picture is seamless. Neighbours: fold (a mirror on one centre seam, a different idea: symmetry), slices (strips that rise one after another; here nothing rises and nothing is separate, it is one sheet that flattens), book (a cover opening and a camera move; here no cover and no move). Buildable with CSS 3D on panel copies of the photograph with background-position offsets.

## 21. The Searchers, the opening shot (John Ford, 1956)
- URL: https://www.filmsite.org/sear.html
- Source: Filmsite's shot description and the standard critical accounts of the shot (the "framed doorway and threshold between the two worlds"). A film opening, not a title sequence; the credits run on an adobe wall before it.
- What: Black, a title card, then a black interior. A cabin door opens from inside, and the doorway is the only light: Monument Valley in full sun, framed by the dark jamb. A woman in silhouette steps into the doorway and out onto the porch; the camera follows her through it, so the dark room falls away and the land is everything. Far out between two buttes a rider is coming. The last shot of the film is the same doorway, from inside, closing.
- Why: The frame within the frame does the work: the dark surround makes the lit landscape burn, and the door is a thing everyone understands, so the reveal is not an effect. Looking out from inside is the stance of his own opening photograph, Aoraki seen from inside a car.
- Quality: the doorway: a dark stage with one opening in it, the photograph seen through that opening in full light, and the opening growing (the door swinging wide, the camera stepping through) until the dark surround is gone and the photograph is the whole window. Nearest excluded design: book, which also hinges; the difference is that nothing here is a cover over the picture, the picture is never behind the door, it is beyond it, lit, from the first frame, and the dark room is what leaves. Also distinct from Rear Window's three blinds (counted panels lifting).

## 33. The soap film in a frame (thin-film interference, drainage and bursting): the colours drain, the black film forms, and it pops
